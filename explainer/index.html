Part I

The big picture, from zero

No equations yet. Just the problem, why it suddenly matters, and the one idea that ties the whole field together.

1. What was broken

Picture yourself reading a long mystery novel. There are two very different ways to keep track of the clues.

The notebook reader. You copy every word into a notebook. Each time you read a new word, you flip back through every page so far, compare each earlier word with the current one, and decide which ones matter right now. You never forget anything, and you can look up any detail exactly. But at word number 10,000 you are doing 10,000 comparisons for that single word, and the notebook gets thicker with every page.

The sticky-note reader. You keep one small sticky note. After each word, you rewrite the note to summarize everything so far. Every word costs the same small amount of work, and the note never grows. The catch: the note is small, so you must constantly decide what to keep and what to erase.

The notebook reader is softmax attention, the heart of the Transformer. The sticky-note reader is a recurrent neural network (RNN). Before 2017, RNNs such as the LSTM were the default. They had two practical problems. They had to be trained one word at a time, which wastes a GPU that wants to do thousands of things at once, and their gradients tended to vanish over long distances, so they forgot. The Transformer fixed both: during training it processes all positions in parallel, and any word can look at any other word directly. It won decisively.

The price of the notebook, though, is quadratic. Word \(t\) compares itself with \(t\) earlier words, so a text of length \(L\) costs about \(1+2+\dots+L \approx L^2/2\) comparisons per layer. Double the length and you quadruple the work. Play with it:

Speed is only half of it. To generate the next token, the notebook reader must keep every past key and value around: the KV cache. It grows linearly with the context and must be read in full for every new token. The sticky-note reader keeps a fixed-size state no matter how long the text is.

2. Why this matters right now

For years the quadratic cost was tolerable because contexts were short: a few thousand tokens. That is no longer true. Models now read entire code repositories, books, hours of transcripts, genomes, and the long trajectories of AI agents that act for hundreds of steps. Contexts of 128K tokens are routine and 1M tokens are shipping.

At that scale the cost of serving a model, not training it, starts to hurt. For an illustrative 8B-parameter configuration (32 layers, grouped-query attention with 8 key/value heads of size 128, 16-bit numbers), the KV cache costs 128 KiB per token. That is 16 GiB for a single 128K-token conversation, before counting the model weights. A recurrent layer of comparable width needs a fixed 2 MiB state per layer, about 64 MiB for all 32 layers, whether the context is 1K or 1M tokens. (Part III has an interactive version of this calculation.)

So this is no longer an academic curiosity. In 2025, several production model families moved most of their layers to linear-time recurrences and kept only a minority of attention layers: MiniMax-01 (one softmax layer per seven linear layers), NVIDIA’s Nemotron-H (about 8% attention), Qwen3-Next and Kimi Linear (three delta-rule layers per attention layer). The rest of this page explains why that recipe works, from first principles.

3. The “aha”, in one picture

Here is the idea that took the field from 2020 to today, told in three beats.

Beat 1: the notebook can be squashed into a matrix. If you remove one ingredient from attention (the softmax), a little algebra shows that the entire notebook can be summarized by a single fixed-size matrix that you update after every token. Attention becomes an RNN. (Katharopoulos et al., 2020.)

Beat 2: borrow physics for good memory. A state that evolves by a linear differential equation, like water in a leaky bucket or charge in a circuit, can be designed to remember for a very long time, and, crucially, can be computed in parallel. That is the state space model (S4, 2022).

Beat 3: make the sticky note smart. Let the model decide, token by token, how much to write and how much to forget (selectivity, Mamba, 2023), and then how to erase and overwrite a specific memory (the delta rule, DeltaNet, 2024). Finally, accept that a sticky note can never match a notebook at exact recall, and keep a few notebook pages around (hybrids).

The thread through everything

Every model on this page answers three questions. What is the memory? (a vector, a matrix, a small neural network). How is it updated? (fixed decay, input-dependent decay, targeted overwrite). What does the update secretly optimize? The history of the field is a sequence of better answers, and each answer fixed a specific bottleneck of the previous one. Once you see that, the alphabet soup of S4, Mamba, GLA, DeltaNet and Titans becomes one coherent story.

Math toolbox 3-minute refresher

Everything below uses only these tools. If they are familiar, skip ahead; if not, open the box. Each item has a tiny example.

Open the toolbox: vectors, matrices, \(e^x\), and three calculus facts

Vectors and the dot product (a similarity score)

A vector is a list of numbers, such as \(\mathbf a=(1,2)\). The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up: \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2\). With \(\mathbf b=(3,-1)\): \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = 1\cdot 3 + 2\cdot(-1) = 1\). For vectors of length 1 (“unit vectors”), the dot product equals the cosine of the angle between them: \(1\) if they point the same way, \(0\) if they are perpendicular, \(-1\) if opposite. That is why dot products work as similarity scores. We also write the dot product as \(\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b\), a row times a column.

Matrix times vector

Each output entry is the dot product of one row of the matrix with the vector: $$\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1+2\cdot1\\3\cdot1+4\cdot1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\7\end{pmatrix}.$$

The outer product \(\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top\) (column times row = matrix)

A column times a row gives a whole matrix: entry \((i,j)\) is \(v_i k_j\). With \(\mathbf v=(2,3)\) and \(\mathbf k=(1,0)\): $$\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\3&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ The one property we will use constantly is associativity: \((\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top)\mathbf q=\mathbf v(\mathbf k^\top\mathbf q)\). On the left, a matrix multiplies a vector; on the right, a vector is scaled by the number \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf q\). Check with \(\mathbf q=(1,0)\): the left side is the matrix above times \((1,0)\), which is \((2,3)\); the right side is \((2,3)\times(1\cdot1+0\cdot0)=(2,3)\). Same answer.

The exponential and its derivative

\(e\approx 2.718\). The function \(e^{x}\) is its own derivative, and by the chain rule \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{at}=a\,e^{at}\). Handy values: \(e^{0}=1\), \(e^{-0.1}\approx0.905\), \(e^{-1}\approx0.368\). The natural log \(\ln\) undoes it: \(\ln(e^x)=x\), and \(\ln 2\approx0.693\).

Three calculus facts

Product rule: \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\). Fundamental theorem of calculus: \(\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t g(s)\,ds = g(t)\): the rate at which an accumulated total grows is whatever is being added right now. Geometric sum: \(1+r+r^2+\dots+r^{n-1}=\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\) for \(r\neq1\).

Sigma notation

\(\sum_{i=1}^{t} x_i\) means “add up \(x_1+x_2+\dots+x_t\)”. \(\prod_{r=1}^{t} a_r\) means “multiply \(a_1a_2\cdots a_t\)”.

4. Attention, honestly

Intuition first. Each token asks a question, the query, and every earlier token holds up a label, the key, and a payload, the value. The query is compared with every key; the best matches get most of the weight; the output is a weighted average of the payloads. That is exactly the notebook reader flipping back through the pages.

Causal softmax attention
$$\mathbf o_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\underbrace{\frac{\exp\!\big(\mathbf q_t^\top\mathbf k_i/\sqrt{d_k}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{t}\exp\!\big(\mathbf q_t^\top\mathbf k_j/\sqrt{d_k}\big)}}_{\alpha_{t,i}\;=\;\text{attention weight}}\;\mathbf v_i$$
\(\mathbf x_t\)
the token at position \(t\), as a vector of \(d\) numbers (its embedding)
\(\mathbf q_t=\mathbf W_Q\mathbf x_t\)
query: “what am I looking for?” (\(\mathbf W_Q\) is a learned matrix)
\(\mathbf k_i=\mathbf W_K\mathbf x_i\)
key of an earlier token: “what do I contain?”
\(\mathbf v_i=\mathbf W_V\mathbf x_i\)
value: “what I hand over if you pick me”
\(d_k\)
length of the key and query vectors; dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores from growing just because vectors are long
\(\exp(\cdot)/\sum\exp(\cdot)\)
the softmax: makes all weights positive and summing to 1, and exaggerates the largest score
\(\alpha_{t,i}\)
how much token \(t\) attends to token \(i\)
\(\mathbf o_t\)
the output at position \(t\): a weighted average of values
Tiny example. Query \(\mathbf q=(1,0)\); keys \(\mathbf k_1=(1,0)\), \(\mathbf k_2=(0,1)\), \(\mathbf k_3=(0.7,0.7)\); values \(v_1=10,\ v_2=0,\ v_3=-5\); \(\sqrt{d_k}=\sqrt2\approx1.414\).
Scores \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_i/\sqrt2\): \(0.707,\ 0,\ 0.495\). Exponentials: \(2.028,\ 1,\ 1.640\) (sum \(4.669\)).
Weights: \(\alpha=(0.434,\ 0.214,\ 0.351)\). Output: \(0.434\cdot10+0.214\cdot0+0.351\cdot(-5)\approx\mathbf{2.59}\).

Where the quadratic cost comes from. Token \(t\) computes \(t\) dot products. Over a sequence of length \(L\), the total is \(1+2+\dots+L\). Gauss’s trick: write the sum forwards and backwards and add the two copies term by term:

  1. \(S = 1+2+\dots+L\)
    The total number of query–key comparisons.
  2. \(S = L+(L-1)+\dots+1\)
    The same sum written backwards.
  3. $$2S=\underbrace{(L+1)+\dots+(L+1)}_{L\ \text{terms}}=L(L+1)$$
    Add the two lines term by term: every pair sums to \(L+1\), and there are \(L\) pairs.
  4. \(S=\dfrac{L(L+1)}{2}\approx\dfrac{L^2}{2}\)
    Divide by 2. For large \(L\), the \(+1\) is negligible: the cost is quadratic.
What attention nails

Because of the exponential, if one key matches the query much better than the rest, its weight approaches 1 and the output is (almost exactly) that token’s value. The notebook supports precise lookup of any past token. Keep this in mind: it is precisely the ability that will be hardest to recover later.

5. Linear attention: the associativity trick

Intuition. What if the similarity score were a plain dot product, with no exponential? Then the weighted sum over the whole past can be pre-added into one matrix, and the notebook collapses into a sticky note. This is the observation of Katharopoulos et al. (2020), “Transformers are RNNs”.

Let’s derive it. Write attention with a generic similarity function, \(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf q,\mathbf k)\), and for clarity focus on the numerator (the denominator works the same way, and many modern models replace it with a normalization layer anyway).

  1. $$\mathbf o_t=\sum_{i\le t}\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf q_t,\mathbf k_i)\,\mathbf v_i$$
    Attention is a similarity-weighted sum of values. Softmax attention uses \(\mathrm{sim}=\exp(\mathbf q^\top\mathbf k/\sqrt{d_k})\), normalized.
  2. $$=\sum_{i\le t}(\mathbf q_t^\top\mathbf k_i)\,\mathbf v_i$$
    Choose the plain dot product as the similarity. (Real models first apply a feature map \(\phi\) to \(\mathbf q,\mathbf k\); the algebra is identical.)
  3. $$=\sum_{i\le t}\mathbf v_i\,(\mathbf k_i^\top\mathbf q_t)$$
    \(\mathbf q^\top\mathbf k=\mathbf k^\top\mathbf q\) is just a number, and a number can sit on either side of a vector.
  4. $$=\sum_{i\le t}(\mathbf v_i\mathbf k_i^\top)\,\mathbf q_t$$
    Associativity: \(\mathbf v(\mathbf k^\top\mathbf q)=(\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top)\mathbf q\). Each term is now a matrix times \(\mathbf q_t\).
  5. $$=\Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i\le t}\mathbf v_i\mathbf k_i^\top}_{\mathbf S_t}\Big)\mathbf q_t=\mathbf S_t\,\mathbf q_t$$
    \(\mathbf q_t\) is the same in every term, so pull it out of the sum (the distributive law).
  6. $$\mathbf S_t=\mathbf S_{t-1}+\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top$$
    The sum up to \(t\) is the sum up to \(t-1\) plus the newest term. That’s an RNN, with a matrix as its hidden state.
\(\mathbf S_t\)
the memory matrix (the “sticky note”), of size \(d_v\times d_k\); its size does not depend on \(t\)
\(\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\)
a write: store value \(\mathbf v_t\) “under” key \(\mathbf k_t\)
\(\mathbf S_t\mathbf q_t\)
a read: ask the memory what is stored under (directions similar to) \(\mathbf q_t\)
Tiny example. Write \(\mathbf v_1=(2,3)\) under \(\mathbf k_1=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf v_2=(5,-1)\) under \(\mathbf k_2=(0,1)\): \(\ \mathbf S=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\3&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&5\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&5\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}\). Reading with \(\mathbf k_1\): \(\mathbf S\mathbf k_1=(2,3)=\mathbf v_1\) ✓. Now also write \(\mathbf v_3=(0,4)\) under the non-orthogonal key \(\mathbf k_3=(0.6,0.8)\). Then \(\mathbf S\mathbf k_1=(2,\ 5.4)\) ✗: the second entry is off by \(2.4 = 4\times(\mathbf k_3\cdot\mathbf k_1)=4\times0.6\). That leftover is called crosstalk.

In general, reading with a stored unit key \(\mathbf k_j\) returns the right value plus a crosstalk term: $$\mathbf S\mathbf k_j=\sum_i \mathbf v_i(\mathbf k_i^\top\mathbf k_j)=\mathbf v_j\underbrace{(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf k_j)}_{=1}+\underbrace{\sum_{i\ne j}\mathbf v_i\,(\mathbf k_i^\top\mathbf k_j)}_{\text{crosstalk}}.$$ Crosstalk vanishes only if all keys are mutually perpendicular, and in \(d_k\) dimensions you can have at most \(d_k\) perpendicular directions. A \(d_k\)-dimensional key space can hold at most \(d_k\) clean memories. See it geometrically below. The memory here has \(d_k=2\) and scalar values, so \(\mathbf S\) is a point in the plane; reading with key \(\mathbf k\) means projecting that point onto \(\mathbf k\). The colored lines are the “correct answer” sets \(\{\mathbf S:\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf k_i=v_i\}\), and a memory recalls item \(i\) perfectly only if it sits on that line.

What linear attention fixed, and what it broke

Fixed: generation costs \(\mathcal O(d_kd_v)\) per token, regardless of context length, and training can still be done in parallel. Broke: (1) nothing is ever forgotten, since \(\mathbf S\) only accumulates; (2) memories interfere through crosstalk; (3) without the exponential there is no sharp lookup. Empirically, plain linear attention trailed Transformers on language, especially on recall.

6. The leaky bucket: continuous-time state space models

Now a completely different route to the sticky note, from physics and signal processing. It looks unrelated at first; by Section 11 you’ll see it is the same equation.

Intuition. A bucket with a small hole. Water flows in at a rate set by the input \(u(t)\) and leaks out at a rate proportional to the current water level \(h(t)\). The level is a memory of past inflow: recent inflow counts a lot, older inflow has partly leaked away. The size of the hole sets how long the bucket remembers.

A one-dimensional state space model
$$h'(t)=a\,h(t)+b\,u(t),\qquad y(t)=c\,h(t)$$
\(t\)
continuous time
\(u(t)\)
input signal (one channel of the token sequence)
\(h(t)\)
state: the water level, i.e. the memory
\(h'(t)\)
how fast the level is changing right now
\(a<0\)
leak rate: the more negative, the faster it forgets
\(b\)
input gain: how strongly the input fills the bucket
\(c\)
readout gain: how the level becomes the output \(y\)
Tiny example. With \(a=-0.5\) and no input, a level of \(h=4\) gives \(h'=-0.5\times4=-2\): it drops 2 units per second. At \(h=1\) it drops only 0.5 per second. A fuller bucket leaks faster, which is why the level decays exponentially rather than linearly.

Step A: no input, pure forgetting

  1. \(h'(t)=a\,h(t)\)
    Set \(u=0\). The change is proportional to the level.
  2. Guess \(h(t)=h(0)\,e^{at}\)
    We need a function whose derivative is \(a\) times itself; \(e^{at}\) is exactly that.
  3. \(h'(t)=h(0)\,a\,e^{at}=a\,h(t)\) ✓
    Chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{at}=a\,e^{at}\). The guess satisfies the equation, and at \(t=0\) it gives \(h(0)\).
  4. \(e^{a\,t_{1/2}}=\tfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ t_{1/2}=\dfrac{\ln 2}{|a|}\)
    Half-life: take \(\ln\) of both sides, so \(a\,t_{1/2}=-\ln2\), and \(a<0\). For \(a=-0.5\), memory halves every \(1.39\) s.

Step B: with input, using the integrating-factor trick

  1. \(h'(t)-a\,h(t)=b\,u(t)\)
    Move the leak term to the left.
  2. $$\frac{d}{dt}\Big[e^{-at}h(t)\Big]=e^{-at}h'(t)-a\,e^{-at}h(t)$$
    Product rule on \(e^{-at}\cdot h(t)\), using \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-at}=-a\,e^{-at}\).
  3. $$\frac{d}{dt}\Big[e^{-at}h\Big]=e^{-at}\big(h'-a h\big)=e^{-at}\,b\,u(t)$$
    Factor out \(e^{-at}\) and substitute line 1. The left side is now a single derivative, which is why \(e^{-at}\) is called the integrating factor.
  4. $$e^{-at_1}h(t_1)-e^{-at_0}h(t_0)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}e^{-as}\,b\,u(s)\,ds$$
    Integrate both sides from \(t_0\) to \(t_1\). The integral of a derivative is the change in the function.
  5. $$h(t_1)=e^{a(t_1-t_0)}h(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^{t_1}e^{a(t_1-s)}\,b\,u(s)\,ds$$
    Multiply through by \(e^{at_1}\). In words: old level, decayed plus every bit of inflow, each decayed by how long ago it arrived.

Step C: discretize (tokens arrive in steps of size \(\Delta\))

Tokens are discrete, so we assume the input is constant during each step: \(u(s)=u_k\) for \(s\in[t_{k-1},t_k]\), with \(t_k-t_{k-1}=\Delta\). This assumption is called the zero-order hold (ZOH).

  1. $$h_k=e^{a\Delta}h_{k-1}+b\,u_k\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_k}e^{a(t_k-s)}\,ds$$
    Apply Step B over one step; \(u_k\) is constant, so it comes out of the integral.
  2. $$\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_k}e^{a(t_k-s)}ds=\int_0^{\Delta}e^{a\tau}d\tau$$
    Substitute \(\tau=t_k-s\): then \(d\tau=-ds\), and the limits \(s=t_{k-1},\,t_k\) become \(\tau=\Delta,\,0\). The minus sign flips the limits back.
  3. $$=\Big[\tfrac{1}{a}e^{a\tau}\Big]_0^{\Delta}=\frac{e^{a\Delta}-1}{a}$$
    The antiderivative of \(e^{a\tau}\) is \(e^{a\tau}/a\); evaluate at \(\Delta\) and at \(0\).
  4. $$\boxed{h_k=\bar a\,h_{k-1}+\bar b\,u_k}$$$$\bar a=e^{a\Delta},\qquad \bar b=\frac{e^{a\Delta}-1}{a}\,b$$
    A linear recurrence, the exact discrete version of the bucket. The bars mean “discretized”.
Tiny example. \(a=-1,\ b=1,\ \Delta=0.1\): \(\ \bar a=e^{-0.1}=0.905\) and \(\bar b=(0.905-1)/(-1)=0.095\). Each step keeps 90.5% of the memory and mixes in 9.5% of the new input. Sanity check: for small \(\Delta\), \(e^{a\Delta}\approx1+a\Delta\), so \(\bar b\approx\Delta b=0.1\). Close!

From one bucket to many. Real SSM layers use a state vector \(\mathbf h\in\mathbb R^N\) and matrices: \(\mathbf h'=\mathbf A\mathbf h+\mathbf Bu\), \(y=\mathbf C\mathbf h\). The derivation is identical with the matrix exponential: \(\bar{\mathbf A}=e^{\Delta\mathbf A}\) and \(\bar{\mathbf B}=\mathbf A^{-1}(e^{\Delta\mathbf A}-\mathbf I)\mathbf B\). When \(\mathbf A\) is diagonal, which is what almost every modern model uses, the matrix exponential is just \(e^{\Delta\lambda_n}\) on each diagonal entry. So an \(N\)-dimensional diagonal SSM is simply \(N\) independent buckets with different leak rates, some fast and some slow, read out together by \(\mathbf C\).

7. The same model, two ways: recurrence = convolution

Unroll the recurrence from an empty bucket \(h_0=0\) (take \(c=1\)):

  1. \(h_1=\bar b\,u_1\)
    First step: nothing to decay yet.
  2. \(h_2=\bar a\bar b\,u_1+\bar b\,u_2\)
    Old content is multiplied by \(\bar a\), and the new input is added.
  3. \(h_3=\bar a^2\bar b\,u_1+\bar a\bar b\,u_2+\bar b\,u_3\)
    Each input is decayed once per step since it arrived.
  4. $$y_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\underbrace{c\,\bar a^{\,k-j}\,\bar b}_{\bar K_{k-j}}\,u_j$$
    The pattern: the input from \(k-j\) steps ago is weighted by \(\bar K_{k-j}=c\bar a^{k-j}\bar b\).

That last line is a convolution: slide a fixed kernel \(\bar{\mathbf K}=(c\bar b,\ c\bar a\bar b,\ c\bar a^2\bar b,\dots)\) along the input. With the numbers above, \(\bar{\mathbf K}=(0.095,\ 0.086,\ 0.078,\ \dots)\). Why care? A recurrence must be computed one step after another, but a convolution can compute every output in parallel, in \(\mathcal O(L\log L)\) with the Fast Fourier Transform. So an SSM trains like a CNN (parallel) and generates like an RNN (constant memory). With several buckets the kernel is a sum of decaying exponentials; with complex-valued \(\lambda\) (a rotating bucket, if you like) the exponentials also oscillate. The catch, and the bottleneck of Generation I

The kernel is the same for every input: the model is linear time-invariant (LTI). It blurs every token with the same fixed filter. That is great for audio, but language needs content-dependent decisions such as “remember this name, ignore that filler word”. A fixed kernel cannot make them. Keep this in mind for Section 9.

8. HiPPO: what should the bucket remember?

A random \(\mathbf A\) forgets quickly or blows up. The question HiPPO (Gu et al., 2020) asked is beautiful: if you must summarize an ever-growing history with just \(N\) numbers, what is the best summary? Its answer: the \(N\) coefficients of the best polynomial approximation of the history so far. Remarkably, those coefficients evolve according to a linear ODE, i.e. a bucket equation with a very specific \(\mathbf A\).

The simplest case, \(N=1\), shows why. The best constant approximation of a curve \(f\) on \([0,t]\) is its average. Let’s show the average obeys a bucket equation:

  1. $$c(t)=\frac1t\int_0^t f(s)\,ds$$
    The running average of the history: our one-number memory.
  2. $$c'(t)=-\frac{1}{t^2}\int_0^t f(s)\,ds+\frac1t\,f(t)$$
    Product rule on \(\frac1t\times\int_0^t f\): the derivative of \(\frac1t\) is \(-\frac1{t^2}\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus the derivative of \(\int_0^t f\) is \(f(t)\).
  3. $$c'(t)=-\frac1t\,c(t)+\frac1t\,f(t)$$
    Recognize \(\frac1{t^2}\int_0^t f=\frac1t c(t)\).

That is exactly \(h'=a\,h+b\,u\), with \(a=-\frac1t\) and \(b=\frac1t\). With \(N\) Legendre polynomials, the same calculation gives \(\mathbf c'(t)=-\frac1t\mathbf A\mathbf c(t)+\frac1t\mathbf B f(t)\) with a specific lower-triangular matrix (indices from 0: \(A_{nk}=\sqrt{2n+1}\sqrt{2k+1}\) for \(n>k\), \(A_{nn}=n+1\), and \(B_n=\sqrt{2n+1}\)). This matrix, plugged into the SSM, gave S4 its long memory. Here is what “remembering the past as \(N\) polynomial coefficients” looks like:

Notice two things. With small \(N\) the reconstruction keeps the gist, a blurred version, and the blur spreads over the whole history uniformly. And as \(t\) grows with \(N\) fixed, each coefficient has to cover more history, so detail is lost. A fixed-size state can only ever be a lossy compression of the past. That theme returns in Section 14.

The story of Generation I in brief: S4 (2022) made this practical with clever linear algebra and was the first model to solve Path-X, a 16,384-step task where every earlier model performed at chance. Then came the simplifications: a diagonal \(\mathbf A\) works just as well (DSS, S4D, 2022), and the LRU (2023) showed you don’t even need the ODE, only a well-initialized diagonal linear recurrence with eigenvalues near 1. The ODE turned out to be a superb initialization recipe rather than the secret sauce.

9. Selectivity: Mamba lets the token set the clock

Intuition. In the bucket, the step size \(\Delta\) is how much “time” passes while a token is being read. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) makes \(\Delta\) depend on the token. An important token gets a large \(\Delta\): a long stretch of time passes, the old water drains, and this token floods in. A filler token gets a tiny \(\Delta\): time almost stands still, so the memory is preserved and the token barely registers. The model learns to choose what to remember.

Why \(\Delta_t\) is a “remember / ignore” dial

  1. $$\Delta_t\to0:\qquad \begin{aligned}e^{\Delta_t a}&\to e^0=1\\ \bar b_t=\tfrac{e^{\Delta_t a}-1}{a}\,b&\to\tfrac{1-1}{a}\,b=0\end{aligned}$$
    Tiny step: the old memory is kept completely and the input adds nothing. Token ignored.
  2. $$\Delta_t\to\infty:\qquad \begin{aligned}e^{\Delta_t a}&\to0\\ \bar b_t&\to\tfrac{0-1}{a}\,b=\tfrac{b}{|a|}\end{aligned}$$
    Since \(a<0\), a huge step drains the old memory, and the state becomes proportional to the current input. Memory reset to this token.

The punchline: selectivity is gating

Take the simplest case: one bucket with \(a=-1\) and \(b=1\). Then \(\bar a_t=e^{-\Delta_t}\) and \(\bar b_t=1-e^{-\Delta_t}\). Write \(\Delta_t=\mathrm{softplus}(z_t)\):

  1. $$e^{-\Delta_t}=e^{-\ln(1+e^{z_t})}=\frac{1}{1+e^{z_t}}$$
    \(e^{-\ln y}=1/y\), because \(e\) and \(\ln\) undo each other.
  2. $$\begin{aligned}1-e^{-\Delta_t}&=1-\frac1{1+e^{z_t}}=\frac{e^{z_t}}{1+e^{z_t}}\\&=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z_t}}=\sigma(z_t)\end{aligned}$$
    Common denominator, then divide the top and bottom by \(e^{z_t}\). That is the logistic sigmoid \(\sigma\).
  3. $$h_t=\big(1-\sigma(z_t)\big)\,h_{t-1}+\sigma(z_t)\,x_t$$
    Substitute both into \(h_t=\bar a_th_{t-1}+\bar b_tx_t\). This is exactly a gated RNN, the core idea of LSTMs and GRUs, now derived from a discretized ODE.
Tiny example. If \(z_t=-3\), then \(\sigma=0.047\): keep 95.3% of the memory and mostly ignore the token. If \(z_t=+3\), then \(\sigma=0.953\): mostly overwrite the memory with the token.

10. The price of selectivity, and the parallel scan

Here is the snag. Once \(\bar a_t\) depends on the token, there is no single kernel any more, so the convolution trick of Section 7 is gone. Must we go back to a slow step-by-step loop? No. Each step is an affine map, \(h\mapsto a_th+b_t\) (with \(b_t=\bar b_tx_t\)), and affine maps can be combined before we know \(h\):

  1. $$h\ \xrightarrow{\text{step 1}}\ a_1h+b_1\ \xrightarrow{\text{step 2}}\ a_2(a_1h+b_1)+b_2$$
    Apply step 1, then step 2.
  2. \(=(a_2a_1)\,h+(a_2b_1+b_2)\)
    Expand. Two steps together are again a single affine map.
  3. $$(a_1,b_1)\bullet(a_2,b_2)=(a_2a_1,\ a_2b_1+b_2)$$
    So define a “combine” operation on pairs.
  4. \(\big((x\bullet y)\bullet z\big)=\big(x\bullet(y\bullet z)\big)\)
    Associativity: the grouping doesn’t matter. Check with \(x=(0.5,1),\ y=(2,0),\ z=(1,3)\): both sides give \((1,5)\).

Associativity means we can combine neighbors in a tree: pairs, then pairs of pairs, and so on. All \(L\) prefix results are ready after about \(\log_2L\) rounds instead of \(L\) sequential steps (Blelloch, 1990; Martin & Cundy, 2018). Watch it happen:

Mamba’s implementation fuses this scan into one GPU kernel that keeps the big expanded state (\(L\times d\times N\)) in fast on-chip memory and never writes it out. That engineering made selectivity practical.

11. Mamba-2: the moment SSMs and attention turned out to be the same thing

Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) simplifies the transition to a single number per head, \(\bar{\mathbf A}_t=a_t\mathbf I\), and then unrolls the recurrence. Something wonderful appears.

  1. \(\mathbf h_t=a_t\mathbf h_{t-1}+\mathbf B_tx_t,\qquad y_t=\mathbf C_t^\top\mathbf h_t\)
    Scalar decay \(a_t\in(0,1)\); \(\mathbf B_t,\mathbf C_t\) are token-dependent vectors.
  2. $$\mathbf h_t=\sum_{s\le t}\Big(\prod_{r=s+1}^{t}a_r\Big)\mathbf B_s\,x_s$$
    Unroll as in Section 7: input \(s\) is multiplied by every decay that happened after it.
  3. $$y_t=\sum_{s\le t}\underbrace{(\mathbf C_t^\top\mathbf B_s)}_{\text{like }\mathbf q_t^\top\mathbf k_s}\ \underbrace{\Big(\prod_{r=s+1}^{t}a_r\Big)}_{L_{ts}}\ x_s$$
    Apply \(\mathbf C_t^\top\) and pull the scalars around.
  4. $$\mathbf y=\big(\mathbf L\circ\mathbf C\mathbf B^\top\big)\,\mathbf x$$
    In matrix form (\(\circ\) is the entry-wise product): a query–key score matrix, multiplied entry-wise by a mask \(\mathbf L\), applied to the values. That is the shape of attention.
  5. \(a_r\equiv1\ \Rightarrow\ L_{ts}=1\) for \(t\ge s\)
    With no decay, \(\mathbf L\) is the plain causal mask: this is exactly linear attention with \(\mathbf q\leftrightarrow\mathbf C\), \(\mathbf k\leftrightarrow\mathbf B\), \(\mathbf v\leftrightarrow x\).
  6. $$L_{ts}=\frac{a_1a_2\cdots a_t}{a_1a_2\cdots a_s}=\frac{\Lambda_t}{\Lambda_s}$$
    The common factors cancel, leaving \(a_{s+1}\cdots a_t\). Every block below the diagonal is (column of \(\Lambda_t\)) × (row of \(1/\Lambda_s\)): rank one.

This is structured state space duality: an SSM is linear attention with a decaying, structured mask. The rank-one structure gives a fast hybrid algorithm. Cut the sequence into chunks; inside a chunk, compute the small attention-like matrix with dense matrix multiplications (what GPUs’ tensor cores are built for); between chunks, pass the compact state. The resulting layer is 2–8× faster than Mamba’s scan and allows states 8× larger or more (typically \(N=64\) or \(128\)). Explore the mask:

Why this matters

The “Transformers vs. SSMs” debate dissolved here. Both are operators of the form \((\text{mask}\circ\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top)\mathbf V\), and the only difference is the mask: dense and softmax-normalized for attention, decaying and low-rank for SSMs. There is also a trade-off to notice. A scalar decay is less expressive than Mamba-1’s per-channel decay, but it runs on tensor cores and so affords much bigger states. Hardware fit has quietly become as important as expressivity.

12. The delta rule: memory that corrects itself

The problem with decay. A decay \(a_t\) shrinks every stored memory at once. Imagine a phone book where the only way to change Alice’s number is to fade every entry a little and then write the new number on top. The old number for Alice is still in there, faded, and it pollutes the read (the crosstalk from Section 5). What we want is to erase Alice’s old entry and write the new one, leaving Bob alone.

Intuition. Before writing, ask the memory what it currently returns for this key. Then write only the correction: the difference between what you want and what you got. That is the 1960 Widrow–Hoff “delta rule”, brought to linear Transformers by Schlag et al. (2021) and made fast by DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024). The cleanest way to derive it is to treat the memory as a tiny regression model trained online, one gradient step per token.

  1. $$\ell(\mathbf S)=\tfrac12\big\lVert\mathbf S\mathbf k_t-\mathbf v_t\big\rVert^2$$
    The goal: reading key \(\mathbf k_t\) should return \(\mathbf v_t\). Measure the squared error.
  2. \(e_i=\sum_j S_{ij}k_j-v_i,\qquad \ell=\tfrac12\sum_i e_i^2\)
    Write it entry by entry. \(\mathbf e=\mathbf S\mathbf k_t-\mathbf v_t\) is the error vector.
  3. $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial S_{ij}}=e_i\cdot\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial S_{ij}}=e_i\,k_j$$
    Chain rule: the derivative of \(\tfrac12e_i^2\) is \(e_i\) times the derivative of \(e_i\). Only the term \(S_{ij}k_j\) in \(e_i\) contains \(S_{ij}\), and its derivative is \(k_j\).
  4. $$\nabla_{\mathbf S}\,\ell=\mathbf e\,\mathbf k_t^\top=(\mathbf S\mathbf k_t-\mathbf v_t)\,\mathbf k_t^\top$$
    The matrix with entries \(e_ik_j\) is exactly the outer product \(\mathbf e\mathbf k^\top\).
  5. $$\mathbf S_t=\mathbf S_{t-1}-\beta_t\,(\mathbf S_{t-1}\mathbf k_t-\mathbf v_t)\,\mathbf k_t^\top$$
    One gradient-descent step with learning rate \(\beta_t\in(0,1]\), which the model also predicts per token.
  6. $$\boxed{\mathbf S_t=\mathbf S_{t-1}\big(\mathbf I-\beta_t\mathbf k_t\mathbf k_t^\top\big)+\beta_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top}$$
    Expand: \(\mathbf S\mathbf k\mathbf k^\top\) groups as \(\mathbf S(\mathbf k\mathbf k^\top)\). This is the DeltaNet update, in the same “transition + write” form as everything else.

Now check that it really overwrites. Assume a unit-length key, \(\mathbf k_t^\top\mathbf k_t=1\):

  1. \(\mathbf S_t\mathbf k_t=\mathbf S_{t-1}\mathbf k_t-\beta_t(\mathbf S_{t-1}\mathbf k_t-\mathbf v_t)(\mathbf k_t^\top\mathbf k_t)\)
    Multiply line 5 above by \(\mathbf k_t\).
  2. \(=(1-\beta_t)\,\mathbf S_{t-1}\mathbf k_t+\beta_t\,\mathbf v_t\)
    Use \(\mathbf k_t^\top\mathbf k_t=1\) and collect terms: a blend of the old answer and the new value.
  3. $$\begin{aligned}\beta_t=1&\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf S_t\mathbf k_t=\mathbf v_t\\ \mathbf u\perp\mathbf k_t&\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf S_t\mathbf u=\mathbf S_{t-1}\mathbf u\end{aligned}$$
    Full overwrite for this key, while directions perpendicular to \(\mathbf k_t\) are untouched (since \(\mathbf k_t^\top\mathbf u=0\)). Erase Alice, leave Bob.
Tiny example (scalar values, \(\mathbf S\) is a row). Memory \(\mathbf S=(2,1)\), key \(\mathbf k=(1,0)\), new value \(v=5\). Current answer: \(\mathbf S\mathbf k=2\), so the error is \(2-5=-3\). Delta update with \(\beta=1\): \(\mathbf S\leftarrow(2,1)-(-3)\cdot(1,0)=(5,1)\). Now \(\mathbf S\mathbf k=5\) ✓, and the other direction \((0,1)\) still reads \(1\) ✓. Linear attention would instead add \(v\mathbf k^\top\): \((2,1)+(5,0)=(7,1)\), which reads \(7\) ✗.

In the plane picture, the delta rule (with \(\beta=1\)) jumps perpendicularly onto the latest key’s line: the smallest change to memory that makes the new fact true. (Numerical analysts know this as the Kaczmarz method.) Additive writes just pile vectors on top of each other. Try the “Overwrite A” and “Three keys in 2-D” scenarios: with three facts in a 2-D memory, no point lies on all three lines, so no update rule can satisfy them all. Remember that for Section 14.

The same lens explains the whole family

Look back at linear attention: \(\mathbf S_t=\mathbf S_{t-1}+\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\) is also one gradient step, on the objective \(\ell(\mathbf S)=-\mathbf v_t^\top\mathbf S\mathbf k_t\), whose gradient is \(-\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\). Linear attention optimizes “be correlated with the values”, which has no notion of error and is unbounded. The delta rule optimizes “be correct”. Everything else is a variation:

13. Negative eigenvalues: why some memories can’t count

So far we have asked how much a state can remember. A different question is what it can compute. Consider parity: read a string of bits and say whether the number of 1s is even or odd. A child can do it by flipping a coin over at every 1. Can our linear recurrences?

Look at the delta-rule transition \(\mathbf M=\mathbf I-\beta\mathbf k\mathbf k^\top\) with a unit key \(\mathbf k\). Its eigenvalues (the factors by which it stretches special directions) are easy to compute:

  1. \(\mathbf M\mathbf k=\mathbf k-\beta\mathbf k(\mathbf k^\top\mathbf k)=(1-\beta)\,\mathbf k\)
    Along \(\mathbf k\), \(\mathbf M\) just scales by \(1-\beta\). So \(1-\beta\) is an eigenvalue.
  2. \(\mathbf M\mathbf u=\mathbf u-\beta\mathbf k(\mathbf k^\top\mathbf u)=\mathbf u\ \) for \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf k\)
    Perpendicular directions are untouched: eigenvalue \(1\).
  3. $$\begin{aligned}\beta\in[0,1]&\Rightarrow 1-\beta\in[0,1]\\ \beta\in[0,2]&\Rightarrow 1-\beta\in[-1,1]\end{aligned}$$
    Allowing \(\beta\) up to 2 unlocks negative eigenvalues, i.e. sign flips.

Why do sign flips matter? Take the one-number recurrence \(h_t=a(x_t)\,h_{t-1}\) with \(h_0=1\). If \(a(0)=1\) and \(a(1)=-1\), then \(h_L=(-1)^{\#\text{ones}}\): exactly the parity. With only non-negative values \(a(1)=\lambda\in[0,1]\), you get \(h_L=\lambda^{\#\text{ones}}\), which only ever shrinks, so even and odd counts are interleaved on a single decreasing curve and no threshold can separate them. Grazzi et al. (ICLR 2025) prove that, with finite precision, linear RNNs with only positive eigenvalues cannot solve parity, and that extending the range to \([-1,1]\) fixes it.

A recurrent form does not by itself grant recurrent power. Richer transitions, with negative eigenvalues or products of several Householder steps (DeltaProduct), buy it back, at some cost in parallelism.

14. The capacity wall: why a sticky note can never be a notebook

The last piece of math is the simplest and, to me, the most important. It is a counting argument (the pigeonhole principle).

  1. A state of \(b\) bits has at most \(2^b\) distinct values.
    Each bit is 0 or 1.
  2. There are \(V^n=2^{\,n\log_2V}\) strings of \(n\) tokens.
    \(V\) choices per position; then rewrite the base: \(V=2^{\log_2V}\).
  3. If \(n\log_2V>b\): two different strings reach the same state.
    More strings than states (pigeonhole).
  4. \(\Rightarrow\) the model cannot copy both correctly.
    After the strings end, everything the model does depends only on its state. Same state, same output, so at least one copy is wrong.
PigeonholesBinary strings (left) must be stored as states (right). Red links mark collisions: two strings that become indistinguishable.

So copying \(n\) tokens needs memory that grows with \(n\). A fixed-size recurrent state must eventually fail, while a Transformer’s KV cache grows with the input. Jelassi et al. (ICML 2024) prove that a two-layer Transformer can copy strings of exponential length, while state space models are fundamentally limited by their fixed-size state. Zoology (Arora et al., ICLR 2024) found the practical face of this: 82% of the perplexity gap between gated-convolution models and attention came from associative recall, and a 70M-parameter attention model beat a 1.4B-parameter gated-convolution model on it.

My reading

The remaining gap between recurrent models and attention is a capacity gap, not an optimization gap. Better update rules (gating, delta, test-time optimizers) use a fixed budget of numbers more cleverly: less crosstalk, smarter eviction. But no rule can raise the budget. That is why the industry answer is not “a better recurrence” but hybrids: keep a few attention layers whose memory grows, and let the recurrent layers handle everything that compresses well.

Part IV

My take

Opinions ahead, clearly labeled as mine. I’ve tried to separate what is established from what I suspect.

18. What I think is still unsolved

Memory that knows its own size. Every model in Part II fixes its state size before seeing any data, while attention’s memory grows without limit. The pigeonhole argument says neither extreme is right for every input. I expect the next real advance to be elastic memory: states that grow sublinearly (say, logarithmically, with fine detail for recent context and coarse summaries for distant context), or whose size the model allocates according to how much it predicts it will need to recall.

A theory of hybrids. 1:3 to 1:7 works, but nobody can derive it. My hypothesis, following the capacity argument: the attention fraction you need is set by the recall demand of the data (how many distinct, exactly-needed facts per context), not by model size. That is testable with synthetic recall tasks of varying density, and I would love to see it tested. Placement (early, middle or late layers), the kind of attention (global vs. sliding-window), and sharing KV caches across layers are just as open.

Transitions that are expressive and parallel. There is a ladder: scalar decay (Mamba-2) ⊂ diagonal (Mamba, GLA) ⊂ identity-plus-low-rank (DeltaNet, DeltaProduct) ⊂ dense (classic RNNs). Each rung adds expressivity and costs parallelism. The search problem is crisp: find transition families that are closed under multiplication (so chunks compose), have compact representations (so chunks run on tensor cores), and include negative or rotational eigenvalues (so states can be tracked).

Evaluation. Perplexity hides exactly the failures that matter here, because a handful of recall tokens dominate the gap. I would want every new architecture paper to report synthetic recall at several state sizes, state-tracking tests, long-context retrieval such as RULER, and end-to-end serving throughput at fixed latency, not just kernel microbenchmarks.

Serving recurrent state. This is an underrated systems problem. A KV cache is an append-only log: you can truncate it, share a common prefix between requests, and roll it back when speculative decoding rejects a draft. A recurrent state is a lossy summary and supports none of that for free. Prefix caching needs checkpointed states, and speculative decoding needs snapshots or recomputation. I suspect infrastructure here will matter for adoption as much as the next architectural tweak.

19. What surprised me

The ODE wasn’t the magic
S4 was sold on beautiful continuous-time math. Then the LRU matched it with a plain, well-initialized diagonal linear RNN. The ODE turned out to be scaffolding: a great way to find a good parameterization, not the reason it works.
A 1960 rule is the 2025 frontier
The delta rule is Widrow–Hoff, older than backpropagation. What changed was not the idea but the algorithm: a WY representation that makes it chunk-parallel on tensor cores. Old math plus hardware-aware algorithms keeps winning in this area.
SSMs and attention are the same thing
I find structured state space duality genuinely delightful. Two communities with different vocabularies (control theory vs. kernels) turned out to be studying the same operator, \((\mathbf L\circ\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top)\mathbf V\), and differ only in the mask.
Less expressive won
Mamba-2 deliberately chose a weaker transition (a scalar instead of a diagonal) to use tensor cores, then used the speed to afford 4–8× bigger states, and came out ahead. Hardware fit is now a first-class design constraint.
Independent convergence
Different labs, different recurrent layers, the same answer: a few attention layers and many linear ones. When independent searches converge, it usually means there is a real underlying law. We just haven’t written it down yet.

20. Where I think this is heading

Speculation, in decreasing order of confidence.

  1. Hybrids become the default for long-context models. The debate moves from “Transformer or SSM?” to “which attention, how much, and where?”
  2. The “memory as an optimizer” view absorbs more of optimization theory: momentum, normalized and second-order inner steps, meta-learned inner objectives. Gated DeltaNet is SGD with weight decay; I doubt SGD is the end of the story.
  3. Distillation becomes the standard deployment path. Train (or reuse) a Transformer, then convert most of its layers into recurrent ones, as in “Mamba in the Llama”, which keeps a quarter of the attention layers and matches the original on chat benchmarks.
  4. Richer transitions return for reasoning. As agents and code models need to track evolving state, Householder-style transitions with negative eigenvalues will move from theory papers into production layers.
  5. Explicit memories become inspectable and editable. A state \(\mathbf S_t\) that is literally an associative memory can, in principle, be probed with keys and edited with delta updates. That is a tantalizing hook for interpretability and control.
The notebook versus the sticky note was never really a contest. The best readers keep both: a sticky note for the gist, and a few bookmarked pages for the details that must be exact.
